Budget guide

How much does it cost to live in Taipei or New Taipei?

The real question is not only "Taipei or New Taipei?" It is rent, commute, first-month setup, and how close you need to be to school or work.

Updated 2026-05-22 Rental and transport sources checked 2026-05-22 Students and expats Budget planning

Short answer

Rent is usually the biggest cost. Taipei City is often more expensive near MRT stations, universities, and office areas. New Taipei can save money, especially for shared rooms, but only if the commute is realistic. Budget separately for your first month because deposit, first rent, temporary stay, SIM, bedding, and basic supplies can make it much higher than a normal month.

Who this guide is for

  • International students comparing dorms, shared rooms, and small studios.
  • Expats choosing between Taipei City convenience and New Taipei rent savings.
  • New workers who need to understand deposit, transport, phone, food, and first-month cash needs.
  • Anyone building a Taiwan budget before signing a lease.

Step-by-step: build a realistic budget

  1. Separate arrival costs from monthly costs. Your first month may include two months of deposit, first rent, temporary accommodation, SIM setup, bedding, cookware, towels, cleaning supplies, and transport card balance.
  2. Choose your housing type first. Dorm, shared room, studio, rooftop unit, family apartment, and serviced apartment are different budgets. Do not compare only the headline rent.
  3. Check commute before choosing New Taipei. A cheaper room in New Taipei can be a smart choice if it is near MRT, bus, or your campus. It can feel expensive if it adds 90 minutes every day.
  4. Ask what rent does not include. Electricity, water, gas, internet, management fee, trash handling, and air-conditioner electricity can change the real monthly cost.
  5. Estimate food by lifestyle. Convenience stores and local lunch boxes can be affordable, but imported groceries, cafes, delivery apps, and western restaurants add up fast.
  6. Add transport and phone. MRT, bus, YouBike, train, taxis, and mobile data should be separate lines in your budget.
  7. Keep emergency cash. Keep money for clinic visits, lost keys, moving, urgent taxi rides, or replacing a broken phone charger.

Typical cost categories to plan

Use these as planning categories, not fixed prices. Real numbers change by neighborhood, season, lease, lifestyle, and current market conditions.

  • Rent: usually the biggest line. Shared rooms in New Taipei may cost less than Taipei studios, but quality varies widely.
  • Deposit: many leases ask for a deposit plus first rent. Confirm the deposit amount, refund conditions, and damage rules in writing.
  • Utilities: electricity can be higher in summer if you use air conditioning. Ask how electricity is charged before signing.
  • Food: local meals, school cafeterias, night markets, delivery apps, groceries, and cafes all create different budgets.
  • Transport: MRT and buses are practical in Taipei and New Taipei. Add regular intercity trips separately.
  • Phone and internet: short tourist plans are convenient at first. Long-term home internet or mobile contracts may require documents.
  • Health and insurance: students, employees, and dependents may have different NHI routes and timing.
  • Setup items: bedding, fan, dehumidifier, desk lamp, power strip, laundry basket, cookware, and hangers are easy to forget.

Taipei vs New Taipei: how to decide

New Taipei is not one place. Banqiao, Yonghe, Zhonghe, Xindian, Sanchong, Tamsui, Xinzhuang, and Linkou feel very different. Taipei City is also not one price level. Da'an, Xinyi, Zhongzheng, Wanhua, Beitou, Wenshan, and Neihu can have very different tradeoffs.

  • Choose Taipei City if: you need to be near campus, office, Mandarin school, nightlife, or a specific MRT line every day.
  • Choose New Taipei if: you can live near a useful MRT or bus route and the rent savings are large enough to justify the commute.
  • Avoid only looking at distance on the map. River crossings, transfers, bus frequency, and last-train times matter.
  • Try the commute once before signing. Visit at the same time you would actually travel to school or work.

Realistic example: A student finds a room in Xindian that is cheaper than a room near NTU. It works because the apartment is a short walk to the MRT. Another student chooses a cheaper room far from the station and spends almost two hours commuting daily. The rent is lower, but daily life feels harder.

Common mistakes

  • Budgeting only for normal months. Your first month is usually not normal because of deposits and setup purchases.
  • Ignoring electricity pricing. Ask how electricity is calculated, especially for summer air conditioning.
  • Saving rent but losing time. A cheaper room can be a bad deal if the commute is long and stressful.
  • Forgetting cash needs. Some landlords, small shops, deposits, or first-week errands may still need cash.
  • Trusting one screenshot from a housing group. Verify the address, building, lease terms, landlord identity, and total monthly cost.
